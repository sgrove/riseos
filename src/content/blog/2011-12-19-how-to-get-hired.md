It's been almost two years since I interviewed for programming jobs in the valley (when it was apparently pretty bad), but I think my experiences are still relevant.

I didn't study CS in university, though I had to take quite a few CS courses for my major (which I never finished anyway). I was previously a self-taught programmer outside of a single C course I took when I was 16.

I got my first 'real' startup programming job based off an irc connection with one of the founders who knew a bit of the work I had done. But due mainly to personality differences, it just didn't work out - and I was stranded in Mountain View with no money, and no work, with a ~2 week ticking clock before I was kicked out of the founders' kitchen floor, where I had been staying.

I went on craigslist and scoured through every post that sounded remotely interesting, and I even had a friend introduce me to a recruiter he really liked (though I've generally found the recruiting business to be a bit distasteful). I sent out at least ~50 emails and had the recruiter send me a bunch of leads.

From there, I got a bunch of answers back, asking for an essay-style initial response, or initial code challenges for screening. And these ranged from obscure brain-twister programming problems to extremely relevant, but hard real-world development problems. I went through and did each one, failing at a few, but generally doing alright, and got invited to do ~40 phone/initial screening interviews.

I asked a few friends (especially a borderline-insane French friend call Müss, who was/is a brilliant programmer) to give me some mock interview problems. One of these question was reversing a linked-list (which I had done as a semester project ~8 years before). I struggled for a bit, solved it in my own time, and felt comfortable.

Then the phone interviews started (using etherpad remotely), and I had one YC company ask me to create a linked-list structure in ruby, and then reverse it. I did it recursively, mentioned that it would blow the call stack, and then re-implemented it iteratively. I also remember there was some problem where I used symbols rather than strings and that would cause some bad memory leaks over time in a long-running process (I don't remember where I would have done that though).

Of course, once you go through ~15 of these interviews, it's no accident when you start to solve the problem in a more and more optimized way. After each failed interview, I'd ask for the right answer, and what they thought their right answer indicated. Learned a lot of interesting tips, and also the right words to casually mention during future interviews.

In-person screening interviews went similar, sometimes with a new question, but generally the same few with a bit of a twist. I started asking if I could assume TCO - usually knowing they would say no, but it would set them back a bit because that's an unusual question. Then, acting as though this was the first time I'd seen this problem, I would ask if it was ok if I thought aloud as I worked my way through the problem on the board. I'd mumble to myself about moving-this-piece-over-here and-now-we're-going-to-get-this, and lo-and-behold, I accidentally solved it in constant memory space, in C - a language I didn't even claim to be particularly good at! Only someone with amazing problem solving skills would be able to do that their first time thinking through this problem so earnestly!

I liked some of the questions I got from my interviews, and I started using them in the later stage interviews. I'd say something like, "I'm really lucky in getting to choose which team to work with these days, it makes for the best environment. So I'm sure you guys wouldn't mind if I ask a few questions to you and see how you think through it and work together as a team, right?" and then toss them one of my random interview questions I had picked up. Usually, they'd struggle through (I wouldn't pick the common ones), and then I tell them, "It's no big deal, you've made some really good progress here and here. This could be done like this though, and with a small tweak, you've solved the problem!" First, knock them out of their frame of mind about their position vis-à-vis mine by setting my own standard I expect them to meet if they want to work with me. Then give them a chance to either fail, which will affect how they see my solving their problem and them failing to solve mine, and help them out kindly so I don't come off as a dick, and we bond a bit. I got some amazing emails from the heads of companies along the lines of, "So-and-so VP eng says we have to hire you - what's it going to take?" from this (which was silly, but flattering). On the other hand, they might solve the problem after a bit, and we'd discuss the different possibilities, and we'd both come out feeling like champs (or I had failed at their question and learned a new trick about how to solve that).

Iterate on this for a few times for the subsequent interviews, then a similar personality-interview with the head of the company (or somewhere up there) where you challenge them a bit, but also try to learn from them (they're the head of what's probably an interesting startup, they probably have something you can learn from them, good or bad).

So, this process, starting from ~50+ raw leads whittled down into ~40 initial phone interviews/coding challenges, which went to ~25 next stage interviews, eventually down to ~6 really good offers, all in about ~1.5 weeks. And from there I was able to play the offers off of one another, until I got the exact job I was hoping to get (at Sauce Labs at the time, which was an amazing experience. I owe a great deal of my current blessings to this experience). Each and every interview was an experiment = given this input, what's the output? How did that vary from the last? What are the typical questions that pop up? Somehow many people who asks these pet questions feels like they're asking it for the first time and are blown away when you solve it properly - which is much easier to do the second or third time around... and certainly the 15th time.

## The tl;dr

If you're looking for a programming position but put off by or nervous about all the irrelevant problems you have to solve in them (reversing a linked list, rats, poisoned wine at a party, etc.), just realize that you'll fail at some, get better, and then start passing. It's a game. Just go through, do some warmup interviews with startups you don't care about, take notes about the questions (you'll be asked them again), practice them and then spontaneously solve them in amazing ways in the next interviews, push back in interviews (remind them how easy it is to fail at these things. They'll be looking for you to go easy on them, and they'll often reciprocate when judging your performance). **DO IT IN MASSIVELY PARALLEL STYLE.** That's the only way, you have to be able to lose a lot of these, and at the end, you have to have enough to be able to play them off of one another.

For people hiring, remember that anyone can pass yours tests with enough experience interviewing, even someone as bad at coding as myself. It's important to add in a 'work with them personally' period - Heroku really does this the right way, from what I can tell, and it shows in the caliber of their team.

## An Idea

Incidentally, I felt like a really cool tool for this would be a resume builder like JobSpice that you could email out directly from, marking which company/position it and it would track the status of each application, with applications that had a chance highlighted in green, and failed ones in red. You could mark "terminated; failed at turtle-and-rabbit pointer problem in 3rd stage interview", or "passed 4th-stage interview about turing-machine dog using a recursive solution" (I loved this question by the way). It would really help with this kind of massive application process, not only in helping to learn what worked and what didn't, but also just to keep track of all the outstanding interviews. It'd also be a goldmine of applicants and what works/doesn't work to give to recruiters if it was done tastefully (opt-in, anonymized, etc.)
